1. What this produces

Adding a puzzle starts by photographing the picture — the box front, or a poster of the motif — before a single piece is laid. That image becomes the reference the solver matches against: photograph a loose piece later, and the backend reports where in this overview it belongs.

Everything below serves one requirement. The overview must be a flat, square-on view of the picture and nothing else. A crop that is skewed, that carries a slice of carpet, or that is stretched by perspective silently degrades every piece match made against it for the life of that puzzle.

Three things stand in the way, and each stage addresses one:

  • Glare. A print under a ceiling light has specular patches that obliterate the artwork underneath. Solved by taking five shots from slightly different positions and healing between them.
  • Perspective. A handheld shot of a table is oblique, so the rectangular picture arrives as a trapezoid. Solved with the camera geometry ARKit supplies alongside each shot.
  • Framing. The picture has to be found in the photo and cropped out. Solved by a backend detector that scores four independent quad proposals.

The one-line summary Five shots are fused onto the centre shot’s viewpoint to remove glare, then the finished composite is warped square-on using the camera pose ARKit recorded at the shutter, and cropped to the region the camera actually saw. The backend then finds the picture’s rectangle within that and warps it out.

3. Stage 1 — guided capture

GlareFreeCaptureController.steps defines five shots: a manual Centre shot, then four automatic corner shots whose anchors sit at unit positions (0.25, 0.32), (0.75, 0.32), (0.75, 0.68) and (0.25, 0.68) of the centre shot. A guide dot is pinned to that spot on the puzzle; the user steers it into a centre ring, and a dwell timer (GlareAimStabilityTracker, tolerance 0.06 unit) fires the shutter.

The offsets are about a quarter of the frame — enough to move a specular highlight a long way across the print, while keeping the whole picture in view.

Where guidance comes from

  • Device — ARGlareGuideSource. An ARKit world-tracking session finds the table. Only the screen centre is raycast — that is where the puzzle is, and the extreme corners of the field of view often have no scene coverage at all — and the hit fixes the surface’s height. The world is gravity-aligned, so the surface is the horizontal plane at that height, and each screen corner is its ray intersected with that plane analytically. A detected plane is preferred; ARKit’s estimated plane is the fallback and is load-bearing, since plane detection over a glossy print can take 30 s or never converge. An estimated hit is accepted only if its normal is near-vertical (minSurfaceNormalY 0.85), so a wall cannot masquerade as the table. Hits beyond maxSurfaceDistance (3 m) are grazing hits on the infinite plane, not a tabletop.
  • Simulator / E2E — GlareGuideTracker. Vision image registration against the reference shot, driven by pusseldebug://previewloop. Its translational results are unreliable on the Simulator and are never trusted for anything but keeping the E2E path alive.

What each shutter press records

captureStill() returns a GlareFreeShot: the full-resolution upright photo, plus a PlaneCaptureGeometry holding the camera transform, the intrinsics restated for that upright image, its pixel size, and the surface height.

Invariant — the surface height is frozen at the reference shot All five shots carry the same plane height, snapshotted from the centre shot’s raycast and never re-measured. Estimated-plane raycasts over a glossy print come and go frame to frame; re-measuring per shot would let that jitter shear the frames apart. For the same reason the world quad is frozen from the pose of the frame the photo actually fired on, not from whatever the live scan reads once the async capture returns.

4. Stage 2 — composition

GlareFreeComposer.compose(reference:others:expectedShifts:geometries:) runs off the main actor and takes a few seconds. In order:

  1. Every frame is EXIF-uprighted and downscaled to a 2048 px long side (workingMaxDimension). Registration runs on 1024 px proxies (proxyMaxDimension), brightness-capped to flat grey so glare carries no gradient for the intensity-based aligner to chase, then blurred coarse and fine.
  2. Each corner frame is registered onto the centre frame with VNHomographicImageRegistrationRequest, from the seed hypotheses below. Refinement iterates up to six passes; a converged warp must additionally pass a direct image comparison (alignmentError < 0.04) or the frame is dropped rather than smeared into the result.
  3. Verified frames are warped with CIPerspectiveTransform and cropped to the reference’s extent — unfilled outside their own footprint, so healing can tell “white because glare-free” from “white because not covered”.
  4. healedComposite applies per-frame photometric gain compensation, builds a robust vote-of-covering-frames darkening map, thresholds / dilates / feathers it into a glare mask, and blends the min-composite in only where the mask fires. Pristine reference pixels survive everywhere else.
  5. Rectifier warps the finished composite square-on and crops it to the region the camera covered.

Registration seeds, best first

SeedWhat it is
Plane The warp the two shots’ ARKit poses imply for anything lying on the table (PlaneRectification.relative). The only hypothesis that models the perspective change rather than approximating it as a shift, and the only one measured by world tracking rather than off the pixels — so the specular patch that can capture the bootstrap has no hold on it. Present only when the burst carries geometry.
Translational bootstrap A translation-only Vision registration on the heavily blurred proxies. Far more constrained than the homographic search, so it lands within a few pixels even where the homographic aligner diverges — but a dominant specular patch can hijack its correlation.
Expected shift The guided flow’s geometric expectation: the shot fired with a known anchor steered to frame centre, so the content moved by that steering distance.
Identity Right whenever the user barely moved between shots.

The verification gate decides between them; a bad seed costs a few wasted refinement passes, never a bad warp.

Rectification and cropping

Rectifier builds the output space from the reference shot’s geometry: the plane region that shot sees, viewed square-on, at a 2048 px long side. The output’s “up” is the reference photo’s own up projected onto the plane, so the result is oriented the way the user framed it.

Invariant — rectify once, after fusion; never per frame before it ARKit reports the plane of the table, but the box stands centimetres above it and the five shots come from five different positions. Warping each frame by the table’s homography therefore leaves each frame’s subject displaced by its own parallax: the burst disagrees precisely on the thing being photographed, and healing fuses the disagreement into doubled lettering and smeared background. Vision’s registration, left alone, finds the plane the content lies on, which is the right one. Rectifying afterwards costs nothing, because two parallel horizontal planes induce rectifying homographies that differ only by a uniform scale and translation — squaring up the table squares up the box’s face too.
Invariant — never fill what the camera did not see Rectifying an oblique frame turns its rectangular footprint into a keystone, leaving wedges along the output edges. Filling them — with white, or anything else — puts a long, straight, frame-spanning, high-contrast boundary into the composite, which is exactly what the lines generator in stage 3 looks for; it will prefer that boundary to the puzzle. PlaneRectification.coveredRect crops to the largest axis-aligned rectangle the camera genuinely covered, so every pixel of the composite is real and the strongest rectangle left in it is the picture’s.

5. Stage 3 — detection and crop

The composite is JPEG-normalized (long side capped at 1920 by ImageUtilities.normalizedJPEG) and POSTed to /api/v1/puzzle/detect-frame, which calls PuzzleFrameDetector.detect_corners and then warp.

Four generators, one score

detect_candidates runs every generator on every photo and scores their proposals on one scale. This is a fusion, not a chain: which generator answered says nothing about whether its answer is right.

GeneratorHow it proposes a quad
mask Colour residual against the background’s chroma — shadow-invariant, since a shadow scales luminance without changing hue — plus a brighter-than-background luminance rule.
grabcut When that mask has real evidence but fragments (a washed-out print under warm light), grows the fragments to the subject’s true colour boundary.
edge Traces closed contours in the Canny map and reduces each to a convex quad.
lines Groups long Hough segments into two roughly orthogonal families, merges duplicates, and intersects pairs. Needs no unbroken contour, so it survives textured backgrounds — and it wins on most real captures.

Scoring

evidence = rectangularity2 × edge_support × border_contrast, and candidates rank on evidence × coverage. Anything scoring below MIN_CANDIDATE_SCORE (0.02) is discarded; if nothing survives, detection returns the full frame at zero confidence.

TermWhat it measures
rectangularity How close the quad’s corners are to right angles. Squared, so a sprawling quad cannot buy its way back with coverage.
edge_support Fraction of the quad’s outline backed by detected edges, in excess of chance — on carpet the dilated Canny map is dense enough that any outline hits edges by luck. The probe window narrows adaptively with edge density (CHANCE_TARGET), and above MAX_INFORMATIVE_CHANCE the term abstains rather than vetoing every candidate.
border_contrast The fraction of each side across which the colour steps the same way, scored as the weakest side. A real object differs from its surroundings in one direction along its whole outline. Taking the fraction rather than the mean also catches a quad that clips a corner.
coverage How much of the frame the quad fills. Ranks candidates only — it is deliberately excluded from the reported confidence, since how much of the frame the puzzle occupies says nothing about whether the quad is right.
Why border contrast is the load-bearing term On a box photographed on carpet under directional light, the cast shadow’s boundary is every bit as long and as straight as the box’s own edge. Both the contour tracer and the line generator will happily build a quad that includes the shadow, and no amount of edge evidence separates the two. What does: the shadow quad steps toward shadow on one side and toward bright cardboard on the others, while the box steps the same way all round.

The warp

warp maps the quad onto an axis-aligned rectangle sized from the quad’s own edge lengths — max(|TL−TR|, |BL−BR|) by max(|TL−BL|, |TR−BR|). This is only correct because stage 2 has already made the composite square-on; on a genuinely oblique input the two horizontal edges are foreshortened by different amounts and this sizing mis-shapes the crop.

6. The plane geometry

PlaneRectification is pure geometry with no ARKit dependency, so it is testable against closed-form answers. Plane coordinates are world (x, z) in metres — the world is gravity-aligned and the surface horizontal, so the plane’s height enters only through planeHeight.

FunctionWhat it gives
uprightIntrinsics The sensor’s intrinsics restated for the upright portrait still: scaled from camera.imageResolution to the high-resolution capture, then rotated 90° clockwise, which sends landscape pixel (x, y) of a W×H buffer to portrait (H − y, x).
planeToImage The 3×3 taking a plane point to a pixel of that shot. A plane is two-dimensional, so the full projection collapses to one matrix per shot.
outputSpace The shared square-on space, sized from the reference frame’s footprint on the plane. Returns nil when a frame corner misses the plane — the horizon is in shot — which is the caller’s signal to skip rectification.
coveredRect The largest axis-aligned rectangle of that space the frame actually covers.
relative The warp carrying one shot’s pixels onto another’s via the plane. Exact only for content on the plane, which is why it seeds registration rather than replacing it.
lowerLeftOrigin, scalingSource, scalingDestination Coordinate plumbing: top-left ↔ Core Image’s lower-left origin, and rescaling a full-resolution warp for downscaled working copies.

7. When something fails

Every stage degrades rather than dead-ends. What the user gets:

FailureResult
A corner frame will not register Dropped from the composite. Four frames still beat one.
No corner frame registers The reference shot alone, still rectified. alignedFrameCount == 0, which the capture view surfaces as “used the centre photo as-is”. There has been no glare removal.
Masked healing fails outright Same as above.
No ARKit geometry (Simulator, or the surface was never found) No rectification. The composite keeps the centre shot’s perspective and the backend crops the trapezoid.
The reference frame’s corners miss the plane Rectification is skipped for the whole burst.
No detector candidate scores above the floor The full frame at zero confidence — the user trims by retaking.
Confidence below 0.4 The confirm screen warns “Detection looks uncertain”.

8. Measuring it

scripts/rectify_quality/ runs the shipped detection and warp over a DEBUG-build capture dump and reports, per image: which generator won, the confidence, IoU and corner error against a hand-labelled quad, the rectangle’s true aspect ratio recovered from the image quad (Zhang & He), the aspect the crop actually got, the shot’s tilt, and the residual skew left inside the crop. Its self-tests validate the metrics against closed-form answers rather than testing the detector.

DEBUG builds dump each burst — the five raw frames, the composite, and the camera geometry — to Documents/GlareFreeDumps/. The scorer reads the geometry to say whether a dump was rectified on device, and takes the real focal length from it instead of assuming a field of view.

9. Known limits

  • Resolution is capped well below what the burst contains. Each shot arrives at 12 MP and is downscaled to 2048 px before anything else happens; the upload is capped at 1920. The five frames are now genuine multi-view samples of one plane — the precondition for rendering above single-shot resolution — but fusion still picks pixels (a min-composite inside the glare mask) rather than combining them, so outside glare the extra four frames contribute nothing. Whether that costs piece-matching accuracy is untested; the cheap experiment is to run the north-star eval with the overview downsampled and see whether accuracy is resolution-limited at all.
  • Confidence is miscalibrated on soft-edged subjects. Poster captures score 0.34–0.44 with visibly correct quads, tripping the “uncertain” warning at 0.4.
  • Low-contrast subjects on wooden surfaces can defeat every generator. One capture in the benchmark set produces candidates scoring exactly zero on every evidence term.
  • No manual corner adjustment. A wrong crop is fixed by retaking, not by dragging.
